## Overview

This tutorial will walk you through building a Raspberry Pi HAT (Hardware
Attached on Top) with a PAM8403 Class D stereo audio amplifier. When you are
done, your Pi will drive a pair of 4&nbsp;ohm speakers at up to 3&nbsp;W per
channel, with hardware volume control, screw-terminal speaker connections, and
software mute/shutdown via GPIO.

## How Class D Amplification Works

Unlike a Class AB amplifier, which keeps its output transistors biased in
their linear region and wastes the difference as heat, a Class D amplifier
works like an incredibly fast switch-mode power supply for audio:

1. The input signal is compared against a high-frequency triangle wave
   (the PAM8403's internal oscillator runs at roughly 350&nbsp;kHz) by a PWM
   modulator, producing a pulse-width-modulated square wave whose duty cycle
   follows the audio waveform.
2. The PWM stream drives an H-bridge of MOSFETs that switches the speaker
   between the supply rails. Because the transistors are either fully on or
   fully off, very little power is wasted as heat.
3. The mechanical inertia of the speaker cone acts as a low-pass filter,
   averaging the switching waveform back into audible sound. This is why the
   PAM8403 is a *filterless* design: no output inductors are required.

The practical result is up to 90% efficiency, so a 3&nbsp;W-per-channel
amplifier can run comfortably from the Raspberry Pi's 5&nbsp;V rail without a
heatsink.

## The PAM8403

The PAM8403 (SOP-16 package, part number PAM8403DR) is a filterless Class D
stereo amplifier. Key specifications from the datasheet:

- 3&nbsp;W per channel into a 4&nbsp;ohm speaker at 10% THD with a 5&nbsp;V
  supply (about 1.5&nbsp;W into 8&nbsp;ohms)
- Supply range 2.5&nbsp;V to 5.5&nbsp;V — perfect for the Pi's 5&nbsp;V rail
- Fixed differential gain of 18.5&nbsp;dB (internally set, up to 24&nbsp;dB)
- BTL (bridge-tied load) outputs: the speaker connects directly between the
  positive and negative output pins, so no output coupling capacitors are
  needed
- Active-low MUTE and SHDN control pins, each with an internal pull-up, so
  the amplifier runs even if your software never touches them
- Built-in thermal shutdown, short-circuit protection, and under-voltage
  lockout

### Pinout

| Pin | Name     | Function                                             |
| --- | -------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | OUT_L+   | Left channel positive (BTL) output                   |
| 2   | PGND     | Power ground (switching currents)                    |
| 3   | OUT_L-   | Left channel negative (BTL) output                   |
| 4   | PVDD     | Power supply for the output stage                    |
| 5   | MUTE     | Mute control, active low (internal pull-up)          |
| 6   | VDD      | Analog supply                                        |
| 7   | INL      | Left channel input                                   |
| 8   | VREF     | Analog reference, bypass to ground with a capacitor  |
| 9   | NC       | No connect                                           |
| 10  | INR      | Right channel input                                  |
| 11  | GND      | Analog ground                                        |
| 12  | SHDN     | Shutdown control, active low (internal pull-up)      |
| 13  | PVDD     | Power supply for the output stage                    |
| 14  | OUT_R-   | Right channel negative (BTL) output                  |
| 15  | PGND     | Power ground (switching currents)                    |
| 16  | OUT_R+   | Right channel positive (BTL) output                  |

:::note
The PAM8403 has **no volume-control pin**. Its gain is fixed by internal
resistors, so volume is handled by attenuating the *input* signal — which is
exactly what the potentiometers in this tutorial do.
:::

## Getting Audio Into a HAT

The Raspberry Pi's analog audio exists only on its 3.5&nbsp;mm jack — the
40-pin header does not carry an analog audio signal. The simplest electrically
correct design is what this tutorial uses: the HAT exposes a 3-pin header
(left, ground, right), and a short 3.5&nbsp;mm-to-jumper patch cable carries
the Pi's line-level audio to it.

The volume potentiometers sit between the header and the amplifier inputs as
voltage dividers, then 1&nbsp;uF coupling capacitors block DC so the
PAM8403's inputs can bias themselves around the internal VREF.

## Building the Circuit Step by Step

### Step 1: Start With the HAT Board

We use the `RaspberryPiHatBoard` from `@tscircuit/common`. It gives us the
standard 65&nbsp;mm x 56&nbsp;mm HAT outline with mounting cutouts and the
full 40-pin GPIO header.

```tsx

export default () => (
  <RaspberryPiHatBoard name="HAT1">
    {/* Components go here */}
  </RaspberryPiHatBoard>
)
```

### Step 2: Add the PAM8403

We represent the amplifier as a `chip` with explicit `pinLabels` matching the
datasheet pinout, and arrange the pins so power/inputs sit on the left side

### Step 3: Add the Audio Input and Volume Control

Each channel gets its own 10k potentiometer as a volume knob. On a
three-terminal potentiometer, pin1 and pin3 are the ends of the resistive
track and pin2 is the wiper, so the signal enters pin1, the wiper taps off a
fraction of it, and pin3 dumps the rest to ground.

### Step 4: Couple the Inputs

A 1&nbsp;uF capacitor in series with each wiper blocks DC while passing the
audio band. The capacitor and the amplifier's input resistance form a
high-pass filter; 1&nbsp;uF keeps its corner frequency well below the audio
band. The datasheet notes that smaller input capacitors also reduce
turn-on pop.

transients, and a bulk capacitor of 20&nbsp;uF or greater near the amplifier
for low-frequency current demand. The VREF bypass capacitor is the most
critical one — it stabilizes the internal analog reference that the inputs
bias against.

A few layout rules that matter for a Class D amplifier:

- Keep C4 (1 uF) as close to the VDD/PVDD pins as possible, with C5 (22 uF)
  next to it. Decoupling directly affects THD and power-supply ripple
  rejection.
- Keep C3 (VREF bypass) tight to pin 8 — it is the most critical capacitor on
  the board.
- Route each speaker output pair (OUT+ and OUT-) together, and keep the
  speaker traces away from the input potentiometer wiring. The outputs carry
  300&nbsp;kHz switching edges.
- The `RaspberryPiHatBoard` outline already includes cutouts that clear the
  Pi's connectors; keep components out of those zones.
- If you hear ticking in an AM radio near your build, add 600 ohm ferrite
  beads in series with each speaker wire, as the datasheet recommends for
  filterless designs.

## Raspberry Pi Integration

### Assembling

1. Solder the 2x20 female header (it comes with the `RaspberryPiHatBoard`)
   on the bottom of the board and attach the HAT to the Pi.
2. Connect your speakers to J2 (left) and J3 (right) — 4 ohm speakers get
   the full 3 W per channel.
3. Run a 3.5 mm patch cable from the Pi's audio jack to J1: tip to L_IN,
   sleeve to GND, ring to R_IN.

### Configuring the Audio Output

Force the Pi to output audio through its 3.5 mm jack:

```bash
sudo raspi-config
# Choose the Audio option and force the 3.5mm ('headphone') jack
# (on some OS versions this lives under Advanced Options -> Audio)
```

Then verify that sound comes out of both channels:

```bash
speaker-test -c2 -t wav
```

Use `alsamixer` to set the Pi's line output level, and the potentiometers to
set the speaker volume. Keep the Pi's software volume moderate at first — the
PAM8403's 18.5 dB of gain means a full-scale input can clip the output.

### Software Mute and Shutdown

MUTE (GPIO25) and SHDN (GPIO24) are active low, so the amplifier plays audio
as soon as the Pi boots. Drive them low to mute or shut down:

```python
import RPi.GPIO as GPIO

GPIO.setmode(GPIO.BCM)
GPIO.setup(25, GPIO.OUT)  # MUTE
GPIO.setup(24, GPIO.OUT)  # SHDN

GPIO.output(25, GPIO.HIGH)  # unmute (play)
GPIO.output(25, GPIO.LOW)   # mute

GPIO.output(24, GPIO.LOW)   # shutdown (~100 uA idle)
GPIO.output(24, GPIO.HIGH)  # wake up
```

## Testing and Troubleshooting

| Symptom                          | Likely cause and fix                                                                 |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| No sound at all                  | Check the raspi-config audio output is set to the 3.5 mm jack, turn RV1/RV2 up, and confirm GPIO25/GPIO24 have not been driven low |
| Sound in one channel only        | Swap the patch cable channels at J1 to isolate whether the problem is upstream or on the HAT |
| Distortion at high volume        | Lower the Pi's software volume; if bass still distorts, upgrade C5 to 470 uF as shown in the datasheet's typical application |
| Hum or whine that follows CPU load | Noise on the Pi's 5 V rail — verify C4/C5 are actually fitted and close to the chip |
| Hiss/tick picked up by nearby radios | Class D switching noise — add ferrite beads in series with the speaker wires     |
| Amplifier gets very hot          | Check speakers are 4 ohms or more and the supply is under 5.5 V; the chip has thermal shutdown but it is not a substitute for correct wiring |
| Loud pop on power-off            | Mute via GPIO25 before shutting down the Pi (the datasheet recommends muting before removing power) |

:::warning
Never short the two output pins of a channel together or connect either
output pin to ground. Each output pin sits at half the supply voltage in
idle, so grounding one puts that output stage into a dead short, and during
playback the two pins swing in anti-phase. Also make sure your supply never
exceeds 5.5 V, the PAM8403's absolute maximum rating.
:::
